# Exercise: URL Shortener

## Prompt

```markdown
# URL Shortener

## Question

Design the link shortening service behind a marketing platform. Customers paste a long URL and get a short link such as `https://sho.rt/Ab3dE9x`; anyone who opens the short link is redirected to the long URL, and the customer sees how often each link was opened.

## Functional requirements

- Create a short link for a long URL. The caller may request a custom alias (`sho.rt/spring-sale`) and an expiry time.
- Redirect a short link to its long URL. Expired or disabled links must stop redirecting.
- Owners can list their links and disable one, for example after it is reported as malicious.
- Click analytics per link: total clicks and clicks per day, broken down by country and referrer domain.

## Non-functional requirements

- Redirects are the product: p99 under 50 ms measured at the server, 99.99% availability.
- Link creation may be slower (p99 under 300 ms) and 99.9% available.
- A short link never redirects to the wrong destination, and generated codes are not sequential or easy to enumerate.
- Analytics may lag behind clicks by up to 5 minutes but must not lose more than 0.1% of clicks.
- A disabled malicious link stops redirecting within 1 minute everywhere.

## Scale

- 200 million new links per month, 5% of them with custom aliases.
- 100 redirects for every link created; traffic is heavily skewed: the top 1% of links receive half of all redirects.
- Peak traffic is 5 times the average.
- Links are kept for 5 years unless they expire earlier.
- Users are global; the service runs in three regions.
```

## Rubric

### Requirements and scope

C1. Separates the create path from the redirect path and treats redirect availability and latency as the primary goal.
C2. States that codes are never reused and destinations never change, and uses that write-once property to justify caching and asynchronous replication.
C3. Asks about or states analytics freshness and loss tolerance before choosing the pipeline.
C4. Names what is out of scope (editing destinations, custom domains) instead of silently ignoring it.

### Estimates

C5. Derives roughly 80 creates/s and 8,000 redirects/s on average, and 5 times that at peak.
C6. Sizes storage for 12 billion links (single-digit terabytes per copy) and concludes storage is not the hard part.
C7. Justifies the code length from the key space (7 versus 8 characters) in terms of collisions and guessability.
C8. Sizes the hot cache and shows it fits in memory.

### API and data model

C9. Defines create, redirect, disable, list and stats endpoints with status codes (404 versus 410, 409 for a taken alias).
C10. Chooses 302 or 307 over 301 and explains the effect on analytics and on disabling links.
C11. Makes create idempotent with an idempotency key.
C12. Keys the link store by code with a conditional insert, and adds an owner index for listing.

### High-level design

C13. Draws stateless redirect servers, a cache tier and a key-value store, deployed in every region.
C14. Keeps analytics out of the synchronous redirect path (buffer, log, stream aggregation).
C15. Includes abuse handling: URL scanning at create, rate limits on creates and on 404s.

### Deep dives

C16. Compares at least three code-generation schemes and picks one with a reason (random plus conditional insert, counter plus permutation, or a key pool).
C17. Handles skew: in-process cache for the hottest links, single-flight on misses, negative caching for unknown codes.
C18. Explains why custom aliases need a single point of uniqueness across regions (last-writer-wins replication would grant one alias twice).
C19. Handles read-after-create across regions (fallback to the primary on a miss before returning 404).
C20. Makes a disable take effect within the 1-minute requirement (invalidation fan-out plus bounded TTLs).
C21. Gets exact click counts from at-least-once delivery plus deduplication, and filters bots and link previews.

### Failure modes and trade-offs

C22. Explains what happens when the cache tier fails and how the store or load shedding absorbs it.
C23. Explains what happens when the event log is down (redirects unaffected, events buffered or spilled to disk).
C24. States the consistency model of each path: strong for creates, eventual for redirects, and why that is safe.
C25. Discusses caching redirects at the CDN and what it costs in analytics and disable latency.

### Operations and evolution

C26. Names the key metrics: redirect p99 per region, cache hit ratio, 404 rate, analytics lag, disable propagation time.
C27. Identifies the main cost drivers (always-on redirect fleet, analytics storage) rather than link storage.
C28. Describes how custom domains or editable destinations would change the design.
